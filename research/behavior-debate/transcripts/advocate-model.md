# Advocate: model-level game-playing agents

## Claim

The behavior loop that turns "plant and water the parsnips" into button presses should be owned, at the bottom, by a **trained game-playing model** — a vision-language-action policy of the SIMA / Lumine / GameScaling family — sitting **behind a DSH tool seam** as the body's "motor cortex", while DSH's LLM keeps everything that makes Player2 a product: goals, persona, permissions, memory, and speech. Every rival architecture pays a per-game authoring tax forever: a new behavior tree per game, a new planner domain per game, a new mode table per game, a new API-mapping layer per game. A policy trained on the human interface (screen in, keyboard/mouse out) pays that tax **once, in the gym, not per game in our codebase** — that is precisely the judge's criterion, extensibility across many games while staying a DSH plugin, because the plugin's per-game surface shrinks to a key-binding declaration (which our body protocol *already requires*, `packages/contracts/src/body.ts` `bodyInputSchema`). I will be brutally honest: SIMA 2 and Lumine are not shippable today, and GameScaling's open weights are a research artifact that needs a GPU. So my actionable claim is two-part: (1) the **seam and tool contract should be designed and shipped now** — a `body_policy` tool the LLM calls with a natural-language subgoal, returning bounded action chunks through the existing body port — because it is small, inspectable, and survives every model upgrade; and (2) as soon as a policy model is loadable (locally hosted GameScaling-class weights today, better ones every quarter), it takes over the real-time control loop wholesale. Design the seam now; the field is moving at exactly this pace.

Measured results I will lean on (all fetched): SIMA 1 was evaluated on **nine commercial games** with ~600 basic skills; multi-game agents **beat single-game specialists**, and an agent withheld from one game **nearly matched the specialist on it** (blog above). Lumine completed the full ~5-hour Mondstadt three-act storyline "on par with human-level efficiency" and, with zero fine-tuning, 100-minute missions in Wuthering Waves and the Honkai: Star Rail first chapter (arXiv + project page above). GameScaling's 27B beats the best specialized Minecraft model by 13 points, and in real-time settings its small models retain ~88% of time-paused performance while frontier LLM agents lose ~90% of theirs (arXiv above).

**How the loop concretely works for "plant and water the parsnips".** The player's words become a DSH goal (seam 5, DSH's `create_goal` — unchanged). The companion LLM decomposes it into subgoals it can verify — "till six dry tiles in the plot west of the house", "plant Parsnip Seeds on them", "water them" — and for each calls the new **`body_policy` tool** (seam 1) with the subgoal text and the body's current view. The tool renders the BodyState snapshot to a frame (on the sim: rasterize the legend grid; on Stardew: the companion seat's own rendered view — [speculation] the SMAPI adapter would need to capture the seat's instance, since today it exports structured state only), sends `{goal, frame, history}` to a locally hosted policy server (GameScaling-class, `policy.act(frame)`), and receives **one 200 ms action chunk**: which buttons held/pressed. The tool translates the chunk into the body port's own `BodyInputStep` vocabulary (`{hold, press, ms}`, ids from `bodyDeclaration().inputs` — the policy never invents button ids; unknown tokens are dropped, as the HF client does), appends the caller's `until` conditions, and submits it as an ordinary `body/input/task-<seq>.json` via `BodyPort.run()`. The loop — observe frame, policy chunk, submit task, check events — runs at the policy's step rate until the LLM's verification pass (`world_find` for watered dirt) passes or the step budget exhausts. Goals, decomposition, verification, and speech stay with DSH's LLM; till-plant-water timing, tool selection, and micro-pathing live in the policy.

**"Get to the ladder" (monsters + low health).** This is where the architecture earns its keep, because it is a *reflex* problem. The LLM sets the subgoal "reach the ladder" with survival constraints as `until` conditions (monsterWithin already exists in `bodyUntilSchema`; a health floor reads from `bodyCharacterSchema.health`). The policy closes the loop at 5 Hz perception / 30 Hz action — dodging the monster, choosing swing-vs-retreat — the class of behavior Lumine demonstrates in Genshin combat and GameScaling sustains in real time where frontier LLM agents collapse. The interrupt path is unchanged from today: `until.monsterWithin` or a health check ends the task early (`interrupted`), control returns to the LLM, which can re-plan or report. The policy is *not* trusted with the decision to stay in danger; it is trusted with 200 ms at a time, inside boundaries the adapter enforces.

## How it fits the DSH seams

Honest framing first, exactly as BRIEF.md demands: a policy model needs GPU inference, so it is a **separate local server the plugin calls as a tool with an inspectable contract** — the same shape as the game's own MCP endpoint, which is already precedent in this architecture (the mod is a local server the plugin calls; `docs/architecture.md`). It is *not* out of bounds, because it owns no goals, no persona, no memory, no queueing — it is an organ, not a harness.

- **Seam 1 (Tools).** One new tool, `body_policy`, defined with `defineTool` like the seven in `packages/dsh-companion-plugin/src/body/tools.ts`, executing under the caller's cancellation (a cancelled turn kills the in-flight policy chunk — the existing `exec.signal` path). Contract: `{subgoal: string, maxSteps?: number, until?: BodyUntil}` → a stream of translated body-port tasks plus a final report. The tool is the *only* new product surface.
- **Seam 2 (System-prompt sections).** Guidance: when to delegate to the policy (real-time control, combat, traversal) vs. act deliberately (`body_walk_to`, menus, shopping) — policies, not code, per the design principle.
- **Seam 3 (Turn-flow hooks).** Unchanged; `[resume]` re-arms goals exactly as today.
- **Seam 5 (DSH goals).** Unchanged and load-bearing: DSH still owns goal state, queueing, cancellation. The policy never sees a goal lifecycle, only subgoal strings.
- **Seam 6 (Body port).** The policy's keyboard/mouse output is *translated into the body port's declared inputs* — the adapter stays a pure adapter, refuses cheat buttons, enforces `until` and step limits. The policy is just a very good author of `body_input` calls.
- **Seam 7 (Local files).** Every policy chunk is already logged as body task files (steps + events) under the bridge directory; a small `policy/episodes.jsonl` beside the memory ledger records subgoal → outcome pairs for replay and audit.

Code placement: translation + tool live in `packages/dsh-companion-plugin/src/body/policy.ts` (~a few hundred lines); the renderer (state → frame) in the same file; the server itself is external, installed like DSH itself is installed (out of the repo, per the runtime boundary).

## Best evidence

1. **GameScaling, miHoYo Honkai AI + THUSI-Lab — [arXiv:2610.28698](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.28698), weights [HuggingFace](https://huggingface.co/THUSI-Lab/GameScaling).** The existence proof that a game company is investing in *generalist keyboard/mouse policies*, open-sourced under Apache 2.0, with the latency numbers to beat: 0.13–0.69 s/step vs 4.0–23.7 s/step for frontier LLM agents on the same games, and small models keeping ~88% of performance in real time where frontier agents lose ~90%.
2. **SIMA 1, DeepMind — [blog](https://deepmind.google/blog/sima-generalist-ai-agent-for-3d-virtual-environments/), [arXiv:2404.10179](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.10179).** Pixels in, keyboard/mouse out, no game APIs, across nine commercial games; multi-game agents beat single-game specialists, and the withheld-game agent nearly matched the specialist — the cleanest published evidence for cross-game transfer of the *interface* skill.
3. **SIMA 2, DeepMind — [blog](https://deepmind.google/blog/sima-2-an-agent-that-plays-reasons-and-learns-with-you-in-virtual-3d-worlds/).** A Gemini-core agent that plays unseen games zero-shot, converses, explains its plan, and self-improves — the direction of travel: one model, many games, language as the contract.
4. **Lumine — [arXiv:2511.08892](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.08892), [project page](https://www.lumine-ai.org/).** VLA at 5 Hz/30 Hz completing a ~5-hour main storyline at claimed human-level efficiency, zero-shot into two other games — hours-long horizon, real-time control, exactly the regime a co-op companion lives in.
5. **Genie 3, DeepMind — [blog](https://deepmind.google/discover/blog/genie-3-a-new-frontier-for-world-models/).** SIMA ran inside Genie-3-generated worlds — the training-gym flywheel that makes "many games" a *data* problem rather than an *engineering* problem.
6. **NVIDIA ACE Game Agent SDK — [dev blog](https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/build-on-device-ai-companions-with-the-nvidia-ace-game-agent-sdk-and-unreal-engine-5-plugins/).** The industry is shipping open-source, on-device, model-driven game agents *now* (PUBG Ally, Total War) — but its acting layer is integration code. That is the counterfactual: model-driven everywhere except the body, which is exactly the seam our approach fills.

## Extensibility across games

The judge's top criterion, head-on. Every rival advocate must tell you the marginal cost of game #2. For a behavior tree: author a new tree. For a planner: a new domain. For the skills advocate: new skill code against a new API. For the modes advocate: a new mode table. All of them re-encode, per game, what a generalist policy learns *once about the interface itself* — and the GameScaling paper names this precisely: an early **"interface phase"** where training brings *broad cross-world gains* as the model learns the keyboard-mouse interface, before gains narrow in a "world phase" ([arXiv:2610.28698](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.28698)). SIMA's withheld-game result and Lumine's zero-shot Wuthering Waves / Star Rail missions are the same finding from two other labs. Our plugin's per-game artifact becomes: the body declaration's inputs and legend — which the protocol *already requires the adapter to provide* — plus, for best quality, a fine-tune we never write code for.

Now the counterarguments, honestly:

- **"Unavailable."** True for SIMA 2 (research preview) and Lumine (no released weights on the page I fetched). Not true for GameScaling: Apache 2.0 weights, a 2.2 GB 0.8B checkpoint on one consumer GPU, vLLM serving, a Python client. It is a research artifact, not a product — but "downloadable and runnable today" is a fact, and the trajectory is one of monotone improvement.
- **"Uncontrollable / unverifiable."** Addressed below — the control point moves *up* a level (goal + until-conditions + step budgets), and the action stream is fully logged.
- **"It doesn't actually generalize forever."** Correct, and GameScaling says so itself: distant-world (34-environment GameWorld) gains did not follow the scaling law and the Minecraft-only 27B *degraded* out of distribution. That is why my claim is a **division of labor**, not a coup: the policy owns the interface skill and real-time control; the LLM owns world knowledge, plans, and verification. The failure mode of a pure policy (world-phase narrowing) is exactly covered by the layer above it.

## Latency, trust, and debuggability

**Latency.** The measured ladder: GameScaling 0.13 s/step (0.8B) to 0.69 s/step (27B) per 200 ms action chunk; frontier LLM agents on the same games 4.0–23.7 s/step ([arXiv:2610.28698](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.28698)); Lumine runs perception at 5 Hz with 30 Hz actions ([arXiv:2511.08892](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.08892)); SIMA 2 trades context for low latency without publishing numbers ([blog](https://deepmind.google/blog/sima-2-an-agent-that-plays-reasons-and-learns-with-you-in-virtual-3d-worlds/)). A hand-written BT reacts in microseconds — it wins the microsecond nobody can see and loses the authoring year everybody can. For a co-op companion sharing a world with a live player, 130–690 ms per chunk with 30 Hz intra-chunk control is inside human-companion tolerance; today's LLM-loop baseline is the slow one.

**Trust — the hardest question, answered structurally.** A pixel-to-input model is uninspectable *as a mind*, and Player2 is a trust product. So the policy is never given trust; it is given **bounds**: (1) the *decision* to act stays with DSH — the LLM calls `body_policy` only inside a player-delegated goal the player can revoke, and a cancelled turn cancels the tool (seam 1's cancellation, already how every body tool works); (2) the policy's raw output never reaches the game directly — it is translated into body-port steps, and the **adapter** (not the model) enforces `until` conditions, step limits, and cheat-button refusal (`bodyDeclaration().cheat`, `sim-adapter.ts` literally fails tasks with `cheat-off`); (3) every 200 ms chunk lands in the existing `body/input/task-<seq>.json` + status/events log — the acting is opaque, the *record* of acting is complete and replayable from files DSH already keeps; (4) subgoal → outcome pairs append to a bridge-local episode log (seam 7), so "why did you swing there?" has an honest answer: the subgoal given, the frames seen, the buttons pressed. Uninspectable weights, inspectable contract, bounded authority — the same deal we already accept with the LLM itself.

**Debuggability.** Failure modes are crisp: unknown button tokens are dropped at translation (fail-closed); a policy that loops hits the step budget and returns `interrupted`; a policy that wanders triggers `blockedMs`/`monsterWithin` exactly as scripted walks do today. The bench compares against the baseline with the same metrics.

## A minimal prototype on the sim

Against `sim-adapter.ts` (imported, not modified), in `bench/`:

- A **frame renderer**: `BodyState` → a small canvas image (legend grid + objects + body marker) — real code, ~60 lines, because pixel policies eat pixels and the sim's native food is the grid.
- A **policy server stub**: an HTTP endpoint implementing the GameScaling client shape (`policy.act(frame)` → one 200 ms action chunk over the sim's declared inputs DPadX/ControllerA/ControllerX/SlotN). The stub stands in for **GameScaling-0.8B served by vLLM** — the real thing is downloadable (Apache 2.0) but needs a GPU and vLLM ≥ 0.17, which the bench does not assume; every number it produces is marked stub-sourced. The stub itself is a tiny scripted policy (greedy walk-to-target + tool-use rules), i.e. the *control-plane plumbing* is real, the *learned control* is stood in for.
- A **`body_policy` tool wrapper**: subgoal string + budget → chunk loop → `SimAdapter.run()` steps with `until` conditions → final report ("watered 6/6 tiles, stamina 240"). Translation and logging are the real deliverable.
- Scenarios: farm (till/plant/water parsnips) and mine-like (ladder, monsters, low health, wrapper extension only), ≥10 runs, seeded; metrics from the shared bench harness (success rate, steps, wall-clock, idle ratio, stuck/recovery).
- What this prototype *proves*: the seam, the translation, the cancellation, the logging, and the porting cost (swap the declaration, not the loop). What it *cannot* prove without a GPU: real learned-skill quality — stated plainly in the results.

## Anticipated attacks and your answers

1. **"None of this is deployable behind a plugin today."** Partially true and shrinking. SIMA 2: research preview, no API. Lumine: paper and page, no released weights found on the project page. GameScaling: Apache 2.0 weights on HuggingFace, one-GPU 0.8B, 130 ms/step, downloadable today ([HF](https://huggingface.co/THUSI-Lab/GameScaling)). The deliverable I actually defend for this repo is the **seam** — one tool, one contract — which is deployable today and gets *more* valuable with every model release. Refusing the seam now means re-architecting later.
2. **"Uninspectable acting breaks the trust product."** The policy holds no authority: goals and revocation are DSH's (seam 5), the tool dies with the turn (seam 1), and the adapter — not the model — enforces until-conditions, budgets, and cheat refusal (seam 6, already in `body.ts`/`sim-adapter.ts`). Every action chunk is a logged body task; the record is complete even though the weights are opaque. We already ship an opaque actor: the LLM.
3. **"Latency is worse than a BT/FSM."** Per single decision, yes, microseconds lose to 130–690 ms. Per *product*, the BT amortizes months of per-game authoring; the policy is 30×+ faster than the frontier-LLM loop you would otherwise run (4.0–23.7 s/step, [arXiv:2610.28698](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.28698)), and holds ~88% of its quality in real time where frontier agents lose ~90%. The right comparison for a co-op companion is the LLM-loop baseline, and there the policy wins decisively.
4. **"You are describing the far future; we ship software now."** The ask is small and present-tense: one tool, one renderer, one contract — guided-not-scripted, per the design principle, which this approach takes *more* seriously than any scripted rival: a learned policy is the ultimate "no fixed behavior code", and the fixed logic that remains (translation, bounds) exists for a stated reason — the model cannot enforce the trust boundary itself. Meanwhile NVIDIA's ACE Game Agent SDK is open source and in beta with shipped titles, and miHoYo — a game company — just open-sourced a generalist game-playing policy. The far future is being pipelined into HuggingFace right now.
5. **"GameScaling's own paper shows OOD doesn't scale."** Yes — the world phase narrows and the Minecraft-only 27B regresses on distant benchmarks. That finding is the architecture's blueprint, not its obituary: it tells you exactly where the policy's competence ends (interface + in-corpus worlds) and where the LLM above it must take over (novel worlds, plans, verification). Rivals cite the same paper to justify *pure* LLM loops, which the same paper measures at 4–23.7 s/step and collapsing real-time performance.
